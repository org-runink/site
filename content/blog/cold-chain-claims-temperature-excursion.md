## What is a cold-chain claim?

{{< direct-answer >}}
A cold-chain claim is a demand for money after a temperature-controlled load was spoiled or put at risk because it left its required temperature range in transit or in storage. It is made against the carrier, the warehouse or an insurer, depending on who had the goods and what the contract says.
{{< /direct-answer >}}

The event behind it is called an excursion: a period when the load sat outside the range it was meant to stay in. A reefer unit that failed overnight. A door left open at a cross-dock. A pallet that waited on a warm dock before loading.

An excursion is not always a loss. Some goods tolerate a short one. That is exactly why the claim has to show what happened, for how long, and against which limit.

---

## What evidence does a cold-chain claim need?

{{< direct-answer >}}
Four things, read together: the temperature range the shipment was meant to stay in, the temperature records for the whole journey, the condition of the load at delivery, and the documents that show who had it when. A claim that shows only one of them is easy to dispute.
{{< /direct-answer >}}

- **The required range,** from the order, the bill of lading or the carrier instructions. If the range was never written down, the claim starts weak.
- **The temperature log,** from the reefer unit or a logger in the load, covering pickup to delivery without gaps.
- **The condition at delivery:** photos at the door, the receiving notes, and any exceptions written on the proof of delivery.
- **The chain of custody:** the bill of lading and handover records that show who held the load during the excursion.

Each of these usually lives in a different system. The log is in a telematics portal, the photo is on a handheld, the range is in the order, and the handover is on paper.

## Why do most temperature excursions end as write-offs?

{{< direct-answer >}}
Because the evidence is scattered and goes stale fast. By the time somebody pulls the log, finds the photo and checks the range, the load has been thrown away and the dock team has moved on. Without the four pieces read together, the claim is easier to drop than to argue.
{{< /direct-answer >}}

There is also a quieter reason. Many teams treat an alarm as the end of the job. The monitoring system flags the excursion, someone takes the load off the line, and the record stops there. Nobody turns the alarm into a claim.

To see this in your own operation, take one month of temperature-controlled deliveries. Count the excursions your monitoring flagged. Count how many led to a claim. The gap is your own figure.
