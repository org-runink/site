Spotting the Illusion: How to Identify Vaporware in SCM Vendor Pitches

Part of the 6-part series on the dangers of SCM Vaporware.

Supply chain software is sold in rooms where nobody can check the claims. The slides are good, the marketing budget is large, and the promises are about what the product will do rather than what it does. Some of those products are vaporware: software that is marketed heavily but does not exist in a working, installable form.

You want to know that before you sign a multi-year contract, not after. That takes a procurement team, a supply chain lead and someone from IT who are all willing to ask the same question twice. This post sets out the questions and the signs.

The Anatomy of a Vaporware Pitch

A vaporware pitch does not look like a scam. It looks like the future.

It is built to work on an executive who is worried about being left behind. The pitch stays on the vision: a supply chain that runs itself, sees disruption coming, and costs less. The supporting material is conceptual animation, a scripted click-through that looks like working software, and a list of the words everyone is using this year — autonomous, self-healing, end-to-end, hyper-automated.

None of that is evidence. The shift a buyer has to make is from wanting the newest thing to wanting the thing you can test.

Red Flag 1: The “Sandbox Evasion” Tactic

The clearest sign is a vendor who will not give you unscripted access to a working test environment.

Most pitches run as a controlled demonstration. The moment to watch is when your team asks to take the mouse, load your own messy data, and try the cases that break things. A vaporware vendor will not let that happen.

The excuses are familiar:

  • “The software is too customized for your industry to show in a generic sandbox.”
  • “We can’t grant backend access — the AI models are proprietary.”
  • “The environment is mid-migration and unstable right now.”

If a product is ready to sell, it can be tested. A vendor who controls every click and will not let your IT team try to break the software in a test environment is showing you a mockup.

Red Flag 2: AI as a Magic Word

Artificial intelligence, machine learning and generative AI are the most abused terms in supply chain marketing. A vaporware vendor uses “AI” to close any question it cannot answer.

So ask the question again, narrowly. If the pitch says the platform uses predictive AI to reroute freight:

  • Which models? Name them — random forests, neural networks, gradient boosting.
  • What was the training data, where did it come from, and how much of it was there?
  • What happens when the world moves away from that training data, or a canal closes?

If nobody in the room — including the sales engineers — can answer, and the conversation keeps returning to autonomous decision-making in the abstract, you are probably looking at rules-based logic with a new label on it.

Red Flag 3: Lack of Peer-Reviewed, Live Case Studies

Working software leaves traces. People use it, complain about it and talk about it.

When you ask for references, watch for case studies about the vendor’s older product, or testimonials that never quite say the customer uses the feature being sold to you.

Ask for three things instead:

  • Direct access to current users. Speak to a supply chain manager at another company, without the vendor in the room, who uses the exact feature you are buying, daily.
  • Proof of scale. If the claim is tens of thousands of transactions a minute, ask to see that volume measured at a live customer.
  • Care with “design partner”. If the vendor says the product is new and you would be a strategic design partner, you are funding their development. That can be a fair deal. It is not the purchase of a finished product, and it should not be priced as one.

Red Flag 4: The Roadmap that Never Ends

Vaporware sells the future. The product today is thin, but the roadmap shows everything you need arriving next year.

Every real software company has a roadmap, so the roadmap itself proves nothing. What tells you something is the history. Ask for the roadmaps from the past three years, and check them against what shipped. Then ask for the release notes for the last twelve months. If the notes show interface tweaks and bug fixes while the roadmap promises a rebuilt platform in six months, the engineering to do that is not there. The roadmap is a sales document.

How to Interrogate SCM Software Vendors: The Definitive Checklist

Five things to do in every evaluation:

  1. The unscripted demo. Sixty minutes, no script, your team driving, your own data loaded.
  2. The technical meeting. Not only account executives. Ask for the architects and the data scientists, and ask them how the system gets its data and how fresh that data is.
  3. Reference checking you control. Cross-check the vendor’s testimonials against Gartner, G2 and industry forums. Find users yourself on LinkedIn and ask how the installation actually went.
  4. The financial check. Look at the vendor’s funding and its spending. Heavy marketing spend with little visible adoption is a reason to ask more questions.
  5. Service levels with teeth. Put uptime, integration dates and feature delivery in the contract, with money attached. A vendor who will not accept penalties for missing a date does not expect to hit it.

Conclusion: Buying on Evidence

Spotting vaporware is a change in how you buy, not a checklist you run once. It means staying skeptical through a good presentation, and treating the buzzwords as decoration rather than information.

Ask for proof that the software works. Test it yourself. Make the technical people answer technical questions. Those three habits are what keeps a budget, and a year of your operations team’s time, from going into something that was never built.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What is the most common sign that an SCM software pitch is vaporware?

The vendor will not let your team use a live test environment, unscripted and hands-on. If all you can get is video and a controlled click-through, take it that the software does not yet work.

How do vaporware vendors use AI to trick buyers?

They use “machine learning” and “generative AI” to cover simple, fixed rules. The method depends on the buyer not asking which models are used, what data trained them, and what happens when conditions change.

Why do legitimate-seeming companies sell vaporware?

Investors want growth and new products. A company may announce something before it is built to test demand, raise money, or stop customers leaving for a competitor that already has the feature.

Can reading release notes really expose vaporware?

Yes. Software that is actively built has a dense, regular history of updates, fixes and small additions. If the platform is described as advanced but the release notes are thin or missing for a year, the product is not moving.

What question should the buying team ask itself after a vendor meeting?

One question: which of the things we just heard could we test next week? Separate the claims you could verify with your own data from the ones that only exist on a slide. Buy on the first list.
