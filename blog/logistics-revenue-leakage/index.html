Revenue Leakage in Logistics: Where It Hides and How to Find Yours

Revenue leakage in logistics is money you were owed, or should never have paid, that nobody asked back. Where it sits, and how to measure yours in a month.

What is revenue leakage in logistics?

Quick answer

Revenue leakage in logistics is money a shipper was owed, or should never have paid, and did not get back. The usual sources are carrier overcharges, bills paid twice, damage and shortage claims left to expire, demurrage and detention on a held container, and orders that miss their window. It is almost always written down in your own systems before anyone acts on it.

Leakage is not one big loss. It is many small ones, each too small to chase on its own. A wrong fuel surcharge here. A liftgate fee for a delivery that had a dock. A crushed pallet nobody had the morning to claim. Each one is cheap to ignore and costly to add up.

That is why it survives. Nobody owns the total. Freight audit sees invoices. Claims sees damage. Operations sees late orders. Finance sees the payments. The leak sits in the gaps between them.

Where does revenue leak in a logistics operation?

Quick answer

Five places account for most of it: carrier invoices that do not match the agreed rate, claims for loss, damage or delay that are never filed, charges for containers held at a port or terminal, fulfilment plans that go stale during the day, and cold-chain loads that spoil without a claim. Each one leaves a record behind.

Carrier invoices. The base rate, the fuel surcharge, the weight and every extra fee can each be wrong. A bill can also arrive twice under two reference numbers.

Claims that are never filed. Loss, damage and late delivery are all claimable, but only inside a window. When building the file costs more than the claim is worth, the small ones expire.

Port and terminal charges. Demurrage and detention run by the day. A container held for one missing paper keeps costing money until somebody clears it.

Fulfilment plans. The plan that was right at six in the morning is often wrong by ten. Orders wait, or ship on a dearer service, while somebody reconciles four screens.

Cold chain. A load that drifts warm overnight is a claim, if the evidence is gathered in time. Often it is just a write-off.

How do I measure revenue leakage in my own operation?

Quick answer

Take one month of records and check every item, not a sample. Compare each carrier invoice with the rate and the shipment record behind it. Count the claims you could have filed and did not. Total the port charges that ran past free time. The sum is your own figure, from your own records, and it costs a few days of one person’s time.

We will not give you a percentage. Any figure quoted before somebody has read your records describes somebody else’s operation. Here is how to get yours:

  1. Pick a month at random, not one somebody flagged as bad.
  2. Invoices: compare every line with the rate sheet and the shipment it bills for. Note each difference and the rule it breaks.
  3. Claims: list every damaged, short or late delivery. Mark which ones were filed, and which are still inside the window.
  4. Port charges: list each container that ran past free time, and why it was held.
  5. Add it up, and split it by cause, not just by total. The cause is what tells you where to fix it.

The split matters most. Recovering a credit fixes one invoice. Finding that one carrier, one lane or one step causes most of them fixes the next hundred.

Who should own revenue leakage: operations or finance?

Quick answer

Both, with one named owner for the total. Field operations sees the cause: the held container, the damaged pallet, the stale plan. Finance and revenue leaders see the cost. Leakage lasts when each team sees only its half, so the total needs one owner and one list that both teams read.

In many companies the chief revenue officer or a revenue operations lead is the natural owner. Leakage is lost revenue, and it shows up as margin. But the fix almost always lands with field operations: a carrier to talk to, a step to change, a rule to enforce.

So the list has to say both things for each item: what it cost, and what caused it.
